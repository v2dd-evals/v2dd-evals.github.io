Clinical AI evaluation / India

Evaluating medical
AI scribes for India.

We are developing an evaluation framework for voice-to-digital-documentation (V2DD) systems across Indian languages and clinical settings. Our aim is to help clinicians, health systems, researchers, and developers assess how accurately these tools turn consultations into useful clinical records.

Motivation

Motivation

Medical AI scribes turn spoken consultations into draft clinical notes for a clinician to review. They offer a way to reduce manual documentation, but their usefulness depends on preserving the clinical meaning of a conversation.

A missed symptom, an incorrect medicine dose, or a statement attributed to the wrong speaker can change the record. Evaluations must establish whether a system captures relevant information accurately and supports effective clinical review.

India needs evidence that reflects its languages, patients, and care settings. Performance on English conversations in another health system cannot establish how a tool will perform in an Indian clinic.

Objectives

Objectives and aims

Our planned benchmark covers 10 Indian languages and Indian-accented English, with separate assessments of speech recognition and clinical note generation.

  • Measure how accurately systems transcribe consultations, including accents and speech that mixes languages.
  • Assess whether generated notes preserve clinical facts, include relevant details, and avoid unsupported statements.
  • Compare performance across languages and specialties using shared data, clear scoring rules, and clinician review.
  • Publish methods and benchmark findings that help developers improve systems and health providers interpret their limitations.
International practice

Lessons from international evaluation practice

Published work from Abridge and Suki offers useful approaches from US clinical settings. The sources include vendor technical reports, a clinical study, and an academic and industry viewpoint. They inform our design but do not establish performance in India.

Abridge: specific checks and continued monitoring

Abridge describes clinician-written rules for individual encounters, detailed checks for note errors, and blinded comparisons between system versions. Its approach combines testing before deployment with ongoing monitoring of clinician feedback and edits. Automated evaluators are checked against expert judgments, including the systems used to detect and correct errors before a draft reaches the clinician. Evaluation overview, encounter-specific checks, error detection and correction.

Suki: assess the quality of the evaluation itself

Suki's 2025 study compared AI and physician-authored notes from 97 English-language encounters across five specialties. AI notes scored better for thoroughness and organization, while physician notes scored better for accuracy, succinctness, and internal consistency. A separate Suki whitepaper questions whether broad ratings using the Physician Documentation Quality Instrument (PDQI-9) reliably detect specific AI errors. Our takeaway is to define errors clearly and test agreement between reviewers, alongside overall note quality. Clinical study, Suki's discussion of PDQI-9.

Evaluate the model, the interface, and clinical use

We adapt the three-level model of Biro and colleagues to distinguish model errors, difficulties with clinician review, and problems arising in local practice. Evaluation moves from controlled tests to observation in clinics. Biro and colleagues, accepted manuscript.

Our benchmark focuses on technical performance. Interface and workflow assessments provide the wider context needed to interpret benchmark results for adoption.

India

Evaluating India's languages and care realities

Languages and clinical meaning

Patients may describe symptoms in a regional language while clinicians use English medical terms. Evaluations need to cover language switching, regional accents, and everyday descriptions of illness.

Each word can express the same clinical concept, fever. A useful note must also preserve whether fever is present or absent, who reported it, and when it occurred. ICD-10 includes R50.9 for "Fever, unspecified", but code selection depends on the documented clinical context. Translation alone does not determine a code. ICD-10 classification.

Different documentation settings

Adoption of electronic medical and health records (EMRs/EHRs) is uneven. Many small clinics still face manual workflows and barriers to digitization. Evaluation should cover settings with limited electronic records as well as established digital systems. Ministry of Health and Family Welfare.

Workstreams

Workstreams

Learn from Indian V2DD developers

Detailed interviews will inform our understanding of product pipelines, supported languages, recurring errors, and deployment constraints. We plan to translate the findings into evaluation scenarios and share aggregate insights.

Build realistic multilingual conversations

We plan to combine appropriately consented patient-doctor recordings with simulated consultations and synthetic data that broaden coverage. Clinicians and language reviewers will assess clinical plausibility and sociocultural realism, including family participation, local expressions, interruptions, and language switching. Real, simulated, and generated data will be identified separately so their limitations remain visible.

Evaluate speech recognition

Automatic speech recognition (ASR), also called speech-to-text, converts audio into a transcript. We will assess word and character error rates (WER/CER), alongside errors in clinical terms, numbers, negation, and speaker attribution. Language-specific scoring rules will account for script and spelling conventions, and results will be reported by language and recording conditions.

Evaluate clinical notes

We will assess factual accuracy, important omissions, unsupported content, speaker attribution, and clinical usefulness. Clinicians will define encounter-specific requirements and assess error severity. Automated language-model evaluators will be tested against clinician judgments. Text-overlap measures such as ROUGE and BLEU will provide supplementary comparisons.

Testing note generation with both verified transcripts and ASR output will help distinguish transcription errors from errors introduced during summarization. We plan to assess notes in English and the consultation language, with separate development and held-out test data.

Resources

Planned public resources

We plan to publish evaluation methods, dataset documentation, baseline findings, and a leaderboard organized by task, language, and specialty. Data access will reflect participant consent and privacy requirements. Reports will identify model versions, sample sizes, and uncertainty so readers can interpret comparisons.
